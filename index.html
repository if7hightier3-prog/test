Why a black box over text usually isn’t redaction

Drawing a rectangle over text in Word, PowerPoint, Preview or most PDF tools doesn’t remove anything — it just paints over the original content, which stays in the file underneath. Select-all and copy, drag the box away, or convert the file format, and the “redacted” text comes right back. It has happened in court filings, law-firm briefs, and repeatedly with the released Epstein files in 2025–2026, where people un-redacted “hidden” text with three keystrokes (Ctrl+A, Ctrl+C, Ctrl+V); an academic study of 11 popular redaction tools — including Adobe Acrobat — found several still leaked.

TrueRedact works differently: it renders each page to raw pixels, burns the black boxes into the pixel data itself, and rebuilds the PDF from those pixels. There is no separate object to peel away because the box is the page. As proof, the app re-opens its own output and searches every redacted page for extractable text — and shows you the result.

      What “Apply” does to your file — be aware

      • The entire redacted page becomes one flat image — not just the black box. Text on those pages is no longer selectable or searchable. That is the safety guarantee, deliberately chosen over surgically editing the PDF’s content stream (which is where most “sophisticated” tools still get it wrong).
      • Pages without any box keep their original text layer. Only pages you actually redact get flattened.
      • For images, re-encoding through the canvas also strips EXIF metadata as a side effect — including embedded GPS coordinates. Most people don’t realize their photos carry location data at all; after TrueRedact, they don’t.

      Honest limits

      • TrueRedact does not auto-detect SSNs, names, account numbers, or anything else. You mark the regions yourself. We left auto-detection out of v1 on purpose: a pattern-matcher that quietly misses one line creates false confidence, which is worse than none.
      • This tool is not a substitute for professional review on legal, medical, or classified documents. It gives you a genuinely destroyed output — but deciding what must be destroyed is still your job.
